Does your online store feel like it is standing still? You publish content, you have product pages, but traffic never takes off. A complete ecommerce SEO audit is the tool that tells you why. Not a superficial audit that lists dozens of points with no priority, but an analysis that identifies the real blockers and the actions that will have a measurable impact.

## What is a complete ecommerce SEO audit?
A complete ecommerce SEO audit is a systematic analysis of every factor that influences your store's visibility on Google. It covers four areas:
- Technical: crawling, indexation, speed.
- Content: product pages, categories, blog.
- Backlinks: the quantity and quality of the links pointing to you.
- User experience: internal linking, bounce rate, conversion.

The goal is not to draw up an inventory of problems, but to prioritize the ones that stop you from reaching your commercial objectives.
Concretely, an audit answers precise questions: why are my important pages not indexed? Why do my product pages not rank for high purchase-intent queries? Why is a competitor ahead of me when their site looks less polished? The audit provides actionable answers, not generalities.

## Why is an SEO audit different for an ecommerce site?
An ecommerce site has characteristics a brochure site does not have:
- Thousands of pages: product pages, variants, categories, filters.
- URL parameters that generate duplicate content.
- Loading times that are critical on mobile.
- Fierce competition on transactional queries.

So the audit has to pay particular attention to the structure of the site, to the handling of navigation facets, and to the quality of product content.

## How do you run a complete ecommerce SEO audit?
Here are the steps we follow for every audit, in order. This method makes sure nothing is forgotten and that the actions get prioritized.

### 1. Technical analysis: crawling, indexation, speed
The first step is to check that Google can crawl and index your pages. We start with the robots.txt file and the XML sitemap. We check that important pages are not blocked, that meta robots tags are correct (no noindex on strategic pages), and that the URL structure is clean (no useless parameters).
Then we analyze loading speed, especially on mobile. A slow site drives visitors away and penalizes your ranking. We use tools like PageSpeed Insights or GTmetrix, but we interpret the results in your context: a site with heavy product images has different optimization paths from a site with a large personalization script.
Finally, we check indexation: how many pages are really in Google's index? We often discover that important pages are orphans (no internal link points to them), or that low-value pages (filters, sorts) are indexed and dilute the perceived quality of the site.

### 2. Faceted navigation and pagination
Filters are what separates an ecommerce audit from any other audit. A category with eight filters (color, size, brand, price) can generate tens of thousands of URL combinations. Googlebot spends its crawl budget on ?color=blue&size=m&sort=price and never reaches your new products. For each filter, the audit answers three questions:
- Does this combination match a real search? "Blue dress size M" is searched. "Dresses sorted by price" is not.
- Crawlable, indexable, or neither? The tools are a canonical to the parent category, noindex, and a block in robots.txt. They do not stack: noindex and Disallow cancel each other out, because a blocked page can never show Google its tag.
- Is it linked from anywhere? A filter page that deserves to rank needs a real internal link, not just a checkbox in a sidebar.

Pagination follows the same logic. If page 2 of a category is only reachable through a JavaScript "load more" button, everything past the first products is invisible to the crawler. Check that paginated pages have their own URL and that they are not canonicalized to page 1, a mistake that erases most of a catalog from the index in a single line.

### 3. Content analysis: product pages, categories, blog
Content is the heart of an ecommerce site. Every product page needs a unique title and an attractive meta description, and content that answers buyers' questions. Many stores use supplier descriptions, which creates duplicate content and does not convince Google to rank you.
We also analyze the structure of the categories: are they organized in a logical hierarchy? Do the category texts bring any value? A complete ecommerce SEO audit checks semantic coverage: for each target keyword, is there a dedicated page? And the blog: is it used to capture informational queries that feed the purchase funnel?

### 4. Product structured data
A product page with no Product block in JSON-LD is plain text for Google. With it, price, availability and rating can appear under your link in the results, in Google Shopping's free listings, and in the product cards AI assistants build when someone asks them what to buy. The audit checks four points on a sample of product pages:
- A Product block with name, image and description.
- An offers block with price, priceCurrency and availability. A price that only exists on screen does not exist for a comparison engine.
- Values that match the page: a structured price of 49 € on a page showing 59 € is a risk, not a rich result.
- Reviews marked up as AggregateRating, when they are displayed and genuine.

Most platforms generate the Product block on their own. Far fewer fill in offers correctly once variants, promotions and out-of-stock states come into play.

### 5. Backlink analysis: quality and quantity
Backlinks remain a pillar of SEO. We analyze the link profile: how many referring domains, what quality (authority, relevance), and above all, are there toxic links that could trigger a penalty? We also compare your profile with your direct competitors' to identify link opportunities.

### 6. User experience and internal linking analysis
Internal linking is often neglected, but it is crucial for an ecommerce site. It distributes authority between pages and helps Google understand the hierarchy. We check that:
- Important pages receive relevant internal links.
- Anchor texts are descriptive.
- The user journey is smooth: no 404 pages, no broken links.

## Mistakes to avoid during an ecommerce SEO audit

### 1. Focusing only on the technical side
Many technical audits list hundreds of problems with no link to business objectives. The result: the site owner is overwhelmed and does not know where to start. A good audit prioritizes actions by their potential impact on traffic and sales.

### 2. Ignoring search intent
An audit that does not take into account the intent behind keywords is incomplete. For example, a product page optimized for "running shoes" must answer a purchase intent, not an informational one. If your content does not match the intent, you will struggle to rank, even with flawless technicals.

### 3. Neglecting internal linking
Internal linking is the cement of your SEO. Without it, important pages stay weak. We often see sites where the homepage points to every category, but where product pages are orphans. The result: they never get indexed, or they never rank.

### 4. Copying supplier descriptions
This is the most common mistake in ecommerce. Duplicated descriptions are a low-quality signal for Google. Every description has to be rewritten, even if it takes time. An audit must identify those pages and propose a prioritized rewriting plan.

### 5. Forgetting the user experience
Google takes into account signals such as bounce rate, time spent on the site, and navigation. If your site is hard to use, visitors leave quickly, and Google notices. The audit must include navigation tests and recommendations to improve UX.

## The angle 90% of audits miss: alignment with business goals
An SEO audit must not be a technical exercise disconnected from the business. Most audits list problems without asking: "will fixing this point increase sales?" For example, optimizing the speed of a page that receives no traffic and has no commercial potential is a waste of time. Improving the content of your most profitable category pages, on the other hand, has a direct impact.
Our approach consists of starting from your objectives: what are your most strategic pages? Which products generate the most margin? Which queries bring visitors who buy? Only then do we analyze the technical or content brakes that stop those pages from performing. This method avoids wasting resources on points that do not matter.
Take a concrete example: an online clothing store. The technical audit reveals hundreds of indexed filter pages. Most agencies would recommend noindexing them. But if those filters match real search queries (for example "blue dress size M"), noindexing them would be a mistake. It would be better to optimize those pages with unique content. That kind of decision is what makes the difference between a generic audit and a genuinely useful one.
Another example: a hardware store. The audit shows that product pages have duplicated descriptions. Rather than rewriting the 5,000 pages at once, we identify the 200 most profitable products and prioritize their rewriting. That is a realistic action with a fast impact.
This business-oriented approach is what separates an audit that stays in a drawer from an audit that transforms your visibility. ## Frequently asked questions

### What is a complete ecommerce SEO audit?
It is an in-depth analysis of every factor that influences your store's visibility on Google: technical, content, backlinks and user experience. The goal is to identify the blockers and propose prioritized actions to improve traffic and sales.

### How long does an ecommerce SEO audit take?
It depends on the size of the site. For a store with a few hundred pages, count one to two weeks. For a large catalog of several thousand pages, it can take a month. The longest part is often the content analysis and the prioritization.

### What are the signs that an ecommerce site needs an SEO audit?
Several signs: a drop in traffic, important pages that are not indexed, product pages that do not rank for relevant queries, a low conversion rate, or simply the feeling that the site is not reaching its potential. An audit is also recommended after a redesign or a platform change.
